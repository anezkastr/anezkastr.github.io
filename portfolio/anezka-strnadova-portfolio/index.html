01 · Multimodal interaction course · presented at the LEGO Group headquarters

Build From Far Away

In hybrid meetings, remote participants tend to feel like spectators. In our icebreaker neither side can win alone: the person in the room feels a hidden LEGO toy with their hands, and the person online tries to recreate it on their screen.

Role
Experience designer, software developer and researcher
Participants
7, both roles each
Methods
Tangible prototype, user study
Tools
D3.js, Arduino, LEGO
Group project with Xinrui HuangWatch the video ↗

Interaction design

Roles that depend on each other

How it plays

  • On site: feel the toy inside a big yellow box, and press a physical LEGO button for each brick
  • Each press drops the corresponding brick onto the remote player’s screen
  • Remote: try to recreate the toy on the screen, arranging the bricks while you both talk it through
  • The app automatically announces whether the recreated image is correct

Key decisions

  • Nobody gets muted: prior work showed silence makes remote players feel isolated
  • We can’t see colour with our hands: bricks of each colour have different shapes
  • Multimodal interaction: an animation and a sound announce each new brick that appears on the screen

Findings and iteration

User study: visuals carried the game, but sound notifications needed improvements

7 / 7strongly agreed the visual cue helped them notice new bricks
6 / 7relied mainly on visuals in the on-site role
8.1–9.3mean ratings out of 10 for enjoyment and clarity
  • In a noisy meeting room, several players missed the soft falling sound announcing that a new brick had appeared on the screen
  • Even a simple apple was hard to rebuild
Change

Replaced the falling sound with a short melody (an earcon) that stands out from meeting noise.

02 · Bachelor’s thesis · Masaryk University

A comic editor for scientists who aren’t designers

People understand complex processes better from comics, but making one is slow and existing tools don’t fit researchers. I designed and built a web editor for creating scientific comics.

Role
Solo designer, developer and researcher
Participants
1 pilot + 10
Methods
Think-aloud study
Tools
HTML, CSS, JavaScript, D3.js
Watch the demo video ↗

Competitive analysis

Every gap became a feature

  • Illustrator can do anything, but it’s paid and slow to learn
  • BioRender has great science images but no comic windows
  • Pixton and StoryboardThat are user-friendly, but built for schoolchildren, with fixed layouts

I designed and developed a fully working web application from scratch.

New functions

Comic windows, a grid of panels generated with one click, speech bubbles, and image upload with a shared gallery.

Usability testing

User study: ten tasks, think-aloud, 40 minutes each

10 / 10completed every task and found the design pleasing
9 / 10said the icons clearly showed what each button does
5asked for undo and redo, the top missing feature

Resizing was not intuitive

Click, move the cursor, click again: this confused almost everyone. People expected to click and drag any corner instead.

Grouping was hidden

The Ungroup button only appeared after selecting a group, which was confusing. Now the button is always visible, just disabled until a group is selected.

Different expectations

People with no graphic design experience expected to select multiple objects by dragging the mouse over them. People with more experience wanted to select multiple objects using Shift.

Iteration

What I changed after testing

ProblemChange
Button purpose wasn’t always clearText labels on hover for every button
Beginners couldn’t select objects to groupA drag-to-select rectangle
Panels couldn’t be dragged by their captionThe whole panel is a drag target
Grey delete buttons looked disabledChanged colour to red
“Add panel” icon was a plain rectangleAn icon that looks more like a comic window

Future work

  • Based on the feedback, resizing objects could be more intuitive
  • Undo and redo functions should be added
  • The application should be tested with more people from more diverse backgrounds

03 · 3D User Interfaces course · Aarhus University

3D Tetris: how strongly should a VR puzzle snap?

Placing objects precisely in mid-air is hard. We built a 3D Tetris-style puzzle, iteratively improved its design to make it more user-friendly and intuitive, then tested different distances for the ghost preview and snapping.

Role
Co-designer, co-developer
Participants
10 regular VR users
Methods
Within-subjects study, Latin square
Tools
Unity, Quest 2, hand tracking
Group project with Kamil KesikWatch the video ↗

Interaction design

Making placement predictable and intuitive

How placement works

  • While a shape is held, its rotation rounds to the nearest 90°
  • Inside the snap distance, a ghost preview appears, showing where the shape will land on the grid
  • A shape that would overlap another turns red and won’t snap
  • A short sound confirms each snap

Changes for the user study

Fully 3D pieces were too hard to finish four times in 20 minutes, so we used flat pieces in a 3D grid.

Study and findings

User study: four snap distances, one puzzle

We tried to find out how far away the Tetris shapes should snap to the grid. We also studied the overall game experience.

Main results

  • 5 cm felt worst: the ghost appeared too late to aim with
  • 10 cm scored best for intuitive and pleasant
  • 40 cm was easiest for one piece, but less precise as the grid filled
  • A medium snap distance gives enough help to feel smooth, without taking control away from the player
  • The game rated 6.5 / 7 for enjoyment

04 · Master’s thesis · Aarhus University

How spatial ability shapes object manipulation in VR

Some people are much better at mental rotation than others. I ran a controlled study to see whether the VR interaction technique (direct vs indirect manipulation) widens that gap, and made design recommendations based on the results.

Role
Sole researcher, designer and VR developer
Participants
22 students
Methods
Pen-and-paper mental rotation test, VR experiment, NASA-TLX
Tools
Unity, Quest Pro, gaze tracking
Watch the demo video ↗

Method

What I built and measured

Study design

  • Spatial ability was measured on paper first, with the 24-item Mental Rotation Test by Vandenberg and Kuse
  • Participants grabbed the orange object and aligned it with the blue grid, using two different manipulation techniques
  • I logged timing, regrips, and hand and head movement

Decisions from the pilot study

  • Controllers instead of hand tracking: a pinch gesture kept unintentionally opening the Quest system menu
  • Easier gaze selection: selection targets a volume around the whole shape
  • Compensating for the delay between looking and pressing: selection uses where the user looked 95 ms before the button press

Findings

User study: difficulty mattered more than technique

51%of variance in manipulation time explained by spatial ability alone
11 / 11split in preference between direct and indirect manipulation
  • Users with good spatial abilities planned the rotation before grabbing; users with lower spatial abilities used trial and error
  • The gap between the two groups grew with rotation size: small at 0° and large at 135°
  • Indirect (gaze-based) manipulation removed the performance gap when picking up the object, but not when rotating it
  • Direct felt more natural; indirect was less physically demanding

Implications

How could designers help people with weaker spatial abilities?

  • Snapping the object once it is near the target orientation
  • A ghost preview of the final orientation
  • Rotation hints showing which axis to turn around (arrows, animation hints, colour change)
  • Live feedback on how close the placement is

For researchers: spatial ability explains so much variance that unbalanced groups could make one technique look better than it is.

Future work

Some participants tried to move the static target grid instead of the movable object. Next time I would distinguish the two objects more clearly: the movable object would announce itself with a short animation, and participants would watch a demo video before the training trials.
